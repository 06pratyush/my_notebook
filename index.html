Proem · The One Rule

This book failed twice before it began — once in Notion, once in Obsidian — and both times for the same reason. They crowned me the librarian. They asked me, at the exact moment a thought arrived, to decide where it belonged, what it touched, which shelf would hold it. That labour is what kills the thought. So this book keeps a single rule, and the rule is a relief: I never file anything.

I write at the back of the tablet — raw, unsorted, mid-breath — and the reckoning belongs to the keeper's machine. It decides where a passage lives, it binds each passage to the others it quietly touches, and it redraws the whole web every time something new arrives. I keep only the right of refusal: when a binding rings false, I strike it. Nothing more is asked of me.

And so the book fills without order and reads without effort — a running commonplace, each entry a small door into the next, its bones borrowed openly from the Zettelkasten method.

There is one threshold in all of it. A passage may be merely held, or it may be truly owned — and between the two stands a trial. To own a thing I must first explain it from memory in words a bright child of twelve would follow; and then, for every plain word I lean upon — random, energy, system — the examiner opens that word like a door and asks me to unfold what lies behind it, as deep as it is asked. Pass, and the passage is mine, sealed and dated. Crack, and it stays merely held, and the crack becomes the next thing I must learn. Nor is a seal forever: each owned passage is summoned back to the threshold when its seal grows old — the doctrine set down under the note on rusting knowledge.

Of Entropy, & the Arrow of Time

№ 1 · physics · thermodynamics · from Thermo 201

The second law says the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Shuffle a new deck and it drifts toward disorder; you will wait forever to see it shuffle itself back into its boxed-up order. That one-way climb is one of the only places in all of physics where time is given a direction — for most fundamental equations run as happily backward as forward.

It is a law of counting, not of machinery: disorder wins because there are vastly more ways to be scattered than to be arranged. The same counting runs beneath the Zettelkasten, which lets the web and not the order carry the meaning — and it stands in strange opposition to Nietzsche's eternal recurrence. The first open question presses on its far edge.

❦

Nietzsche's Eternal Recurrence

№ 2 · philosophy · The Gay Science §341

A demon comes to you in your loneliest hour and says you will live this exact life again, in every last detail, without end. The point is not cosmology; it is a test held up to the way you live. If the thought is unbearable, something must change. If you could want it — want every ache of it, again and forever — then you have come near what he means by saying yes to life.

It sits in strange tension with entropy's one-way arrow: a universe that only ever slides toward disorder does not obviously leave room for anything to return and repeat.

The Zettelkasten Method

№ 3 · methodology · on keeping notes

Niklas Luhmann kept some ninety thousand index cards across his life, and from them wrote more than seventy books. Each note held one idea, wore a permanent number, and was bound directly to the others it touched — no folders, no forced hierarchy. The web and the index made it navigable; the physical order of the cards scarcely mattered at all.

This book is that idea, made to run: passages instead of cards, a tap instead of a number pencilled in the margin, and whatever is linked reachable at once — one long book in place of a wooden cabinet. It is what the making of this book leans on for its bones, and what the second open question quietly disputes.

The Making of This Book

№ 4 · architect & sole keeper · begun this July

One book to hold everything — that is the whole of the ambition — and to make the line between merely knowing a thing and truly owning it a real border rather than a hope. It leans on the Zettelkasten method for its bones, and adds two things Luhmann never had: a guarded threshold, and a keeper who files without complaint or fatigue.

What remains is simple and endless. Write at the back of the tablet without sorting a single line; let the keeper carry each scrap to its rightful place and bind it; and cross one held passage at a time over the border by trial — knowing the seal itself grows old, as the note on decay demands, and that the note on drawing would add a second, wordless half to every examination.

On the Decay of What We Own

№ 5 · caught the sixteenth of July

Knowledge rusts. A passage that passed its trial a year ago might not pass it today. So let every owned thing be summoned back to the threshold each half-year; should it fail, let it fall again to merely held, its old stamp and all. Ownership then is a tribute paid over and over, never a deed signed once — the book made to fight its own entropy, and the promise already kept in the making of this book.

❦

Draw It, or You Don't Own It

№ 6 · caught the sixteenth of July

A passage is not fully owned until it carries one diagram drawn from memory, by hand — no tracing, no reference. The hand refuses to bluff where the tongue will happily go on. If you cannot draw the shape of a thing, you have not yet understood its shape; and so the drawing becomes the quietest, most honest half of the trial — and the tablet's pen its natural instrument.

Does the arrow of time flatten out?

№ 7 · an open question · physics

If entropy only ever climbs, does an expanding universe at last run out of climb — the long cold they call heat death — and once nothing more can change, does "the direction of time" still mean anything at all? The question feeds straight back into entropy; answering it well is likely what that passage's next trial will demand.

❦

What is the smallest thing worth its own page?

№ 8 · an open question · method

A single fact? A bare definition? Luhmann asked one idea to a card — but is a lone number an idea at all? Somewhere there is a floor, below which a thing belongs folded inside an existing passage rather than standing alone with a page of its own. I do not yet know where that floor lies.

Of the Heart, & Its Four Chambers

№ 9 · biology · anatomy · drawn from memory

The heart is a pair of pumps built into one muscle, tucked behind the breastbone and set slightly to the left. The right half receives blue, tired blood from the whole body and pushes it a short way into the lungs; the left half receives the freshly reddened blood coming back and pushes it — with much greater force — out into the whole body. Two pumps, two loops, one steady beat.

Each half has an upper room (the atrium, a soft holding chamber) and a lower room (the ventricle, a thick-walled forcing chamber). Between them lies a one-way valve, and between the ventricles and the great vessels lies another. Four chambers, four valves, and a wall — the septum — that keeps the blue and the red apart forever. The whole apparatus sits in a slippery sac and beats about a hundred thousand times a day without asking.

The whole loop takes less than a minute at rest: blue blood returns through the great veins into the right atrium, drops past the tricuspid into the right ventricle, is pushed through the pulmonary valve to the lungs, comes back reddened through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium, drops past the mitral into the left ventricle, and is thrown — with the loudest beat of the whole system — through the aortic valve and out into the aorta. In a strange way the heart quietly opposes the second law: it spends energy, ceaselessly, to keep the body's chemistry from smoothing into equilibrium.
